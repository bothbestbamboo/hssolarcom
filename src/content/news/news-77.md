<h2>The Shift Toward Higher Power</h2><p>Utility scale solar has moved steadily toward higher power per module over the past several years, and the 700 watt plus class has become the working standard for new large plants rather than a premium niche. The shift did not happen because of a single cell technology. It came from larger silicon wafers, cell architectures that capture more of the light spectrum, and module designs that pack more active area into each panel. Together these changes pushed the nameplate power of a single module from the low 400 watt range common a few years ago into the 700 watt and above band that now anchors most utility tenders.</p><p>For developers, the appeal is straightforward. A plant built from higher power modules needs fewer modules to reach the same capacity, and fewer modules means fewer of almost everything that costs money on site, from mounting rails and clamps to cabling runs and the labour of bolting each panel down. The module itself is only one line item in a utility budget, and the balance of system often matters more than the price per watt of the panel. Higher power modules attack that balance of system directly, which is why they have moved from a marketing claim into the default specification for serious utility work.</p><h2>Where the Real Savings Come From</h2><p>The cost case for 700 watt plus modules is built on balance of system savings rather than on the cell itself, and understanding that distinction matters when comparing quotes. The module price per watt may be similar to, or even slightly higher than, a lower power alternative, but the installed cost per watt across the whole plant tends to move in the buyer's favour once everything else is counted.</p><p>Mounting structure is one of the clearest examples. A plant built with 540 watt modules needs many more panels than one built with 700 watt modules at the same capacity. That is tens of thousands fewer panels to clamp, rail, and ground, and the structural steel that supports them shrinks accordingly. Tracker suppliers design their tube lengths and torque ratings around module counts, so a drop in panel count flows straight into less steel, fewer foundations, and a shorter assembly time on site.</p><p>Cabling tells a similar story. Fewer modules mean fewer string connections, shorter DC harness runs, and fewer combiner boxes. String voltages can be pushed higher within inverter limits, which reduces the number of strings needed to reach a given capacity and trims the DC wiring cost. Labour is the third lever. Every panel handled on site is a fixed unit of work, from unloading and staging to final torque checking. Reducing the panel count by close to a quarter reduces the handling and installation labour in rough proportion, and that saving is felt most in markets where field labour is expensive or where skilled crews are scarce during peak construction windows.</p><h2>Land Use and Plant Layout</h2><p>Beyond equipment and labour, higher power modules change how a project fits its land, and land is often the constraint that decides whether a site is viable at all. A site that can host a fixed capacity in fewer rows and fewer panels can either be built smaller, freeing land for other use, or built to the same footprint at higher capacity, improving the megawatts per hectare that the project delivers.</p><p>This matters most in markets where land is expensive, contested, or simply limited by terrain. A hillside site with irregular boundaries can absorb a higher power module more gracefully because fewer rows need to fit the contours, and the layout has more flexibility to work around slopes, drainage lines, and setbacks. Fewer, higher output panels give the designer more freedom to route rows and access tracks without wasting land that would otherwise sit idle. Where a grid connection point allows a certain export capacity, a project that reaches that capacity with fewer modules and shorter string runs can land closer to its nameplate in real conditions, because the plant spends less energy in wiring losses and mismatch.</p><h2>Energy Yield, Not Just Nameplate</h2><p>Nameplate power is the figure that sells a module, but yield is what pays back the plant, and the 700 watt plus class has had to prove itself on yield as much as on cost. The early concern with very high power modules was heat. Larger cells and higher current can raise the operating temperature and the thermal losses, and a module that is powerful on paper but runs hot in the field gives back some of its advantage.</p><p>The current generation of 700 watt plus designs has addressed this through parallel moves. Lower temperature coefficients, meaning the panel loses less output for each degree it heats up, reduce the penalty for running warm. Better thermal management through frame and backsheet design helps dissipate heat. And the move to cell architectures that split the current path, such as half cell and multi busbar layouts, keeps resistive losses in check even as the total power rises. The result is that a well engineered 700 watt plus module tends to hold onto its advantage through the hot middle of the day when utility plants earn much of their revenue.</p><p>Bifaciality is the other yield lever that large format modules lean on. A high power module with a transparent backside captures light reflected from the ground behind the array, and the gain is most useful on the flat, high albedo surfaces, like concrete, sand, or light gravel, that utility sites often provide. The combination of a strong front side and a meaningful rear contribution is what allows these modules to deliver energy yields that justify their selection over lower power alternatives.</p><h2>Handling and Site Reality</h2><p>Higher power modules are larger and heavier, and that is a real trade-off that gets less attention than the cost savings. A 700 watt plus panel is not a one person lift in the way a smaller residential module can be, and the logistics chain from factory to site has to be planned around that. Container packing density, truck loading, and the equipment used to move panels across a site all need to account for the larger footprint.</p><p>On site, the handling question shows up in crew composition and the staging plan. Teams often work in pairs for large format modules, and the sequence of unloading, staging, and mounting has to keep installers fed with panels at a steady rate without leaving stacks exposed to wind that can damage edges. Tracker suppliers have responded with clamps and torque tube spacing matched to the larger modules, and mounting hardware has evolved to make the physical act of fixing a heavier panel manageable, but the site plan has to respect the weight and the wind area from the start. A project that buys the highest power module available but does not adjust its logistics, mounting, and crew plan to suit will give back some of the saving in damaged goods and slow installation. The projects that benefit most are the ones that take the larger module as a given and design the whole construction sequence around it.</p><h2>Reliability Over a Long Asset Life</h2><p>Utility solar plants are built to run for twenty five to thirty years, and the financial case for any module rests on it holding its output across that life. The 700 watt plus class has faced reasonable scrutiny here, because higher current and larger glass areas put more stress on cells, connectors, and sealing than the lower power designs that came before. The question is whether the savings on day one survive the decades of operation that follow.</p><p>Manufacturers have answered this with a mix of design and testing changes. Thicker front glass, often 3.2 millimetres or more on the larger formats, gives the panel the stiffness to resist wind and snow loads across a bigger area. Improved encapsulants and backsheets slow the moisture and UV ageing that quietly erode output over time. Junction boxes on high power modules are rated for higher current and are often split into multiple units to spread heat and reduce the risk of a single point failure. Independent testing has followed the same direction, with sequential stress testing that stacks damp heat, thermal cycling, and mechanical load to mimic years of field ageing in a shorter period. Panels that pass these stacked sequences give developers more confidence that the higher current of a 700 watt plus design will not translate into higher degradation in the field, and that confidence is what allows lenders and insurers to back plants built around the new class.</p><h2>How Procurement Is Changing</h2><p>The dominance of 700 watt plus modules is visible in how utility tenders are written and evaluated. A few years ago, module power class was a line item that buyers compared alongside price and warranty. In many of the larger markets it has now become a baseline expectation, with tenders setting a minimum power threshold that effectively rules out lower output panels regardless of their price advantage. Procurement teams that once optimised on price per watt now run their models on installed cost per watt and lifetime levelised cost, and on both measures the higher power class tends to win.</p><p>That does not mean every project should take the highest power module available. Small sites, rooftops, and projects with awkward access or strict weight limits may still be better served by a mid power panel that is easier to handle. But for the flat, large scale utility ground mounts that make up the bulk of new solar capacity, the economics, the layout, and the yield story all point in the same direction. The 700 watt plus module is no longer a leading edge choice, it is the format the industry has settled on, and the question for most utility developers is not whether to use it but how to design the rest of the plant to make the most of it.</p>
